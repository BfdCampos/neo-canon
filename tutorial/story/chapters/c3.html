The fog lifted at the sixth mile all at once, like a sheet pulled off the furniture in a shut-up house, and there was Marrowby on its little hill: the church, the mill, the long roofs of the wool market, and the gulls wheeling over all of it as if they owned the place. They probably did.

Juniper put on speed for the last mile, because she knew there was a stable at the Bell with oats in it, and because she had opinions about fog that were best expressed at a trot. Wren let her go. Her own hands had stopped belonging to her somewhere around the Long Bank.

By the time they came up the hill, the lamps of Marowby were being lit one by one along the high street, and the Bell had its shutters open and a fire showing red behind the glass.

Mistress Odile Fenn kept the counting house beside the wool market. She was a tall woman with a tall woman’s way of looking down at a letter as if it had personally disappointed her. She read this one standing at the window, while her clerk pretended to write in a ledger and did not write anything at all.

Wren’s brown eyes went from the clerk to the clock and back again. It was a quarter to six. The tide would turn at seven, and she meant to be on the right side of it when it did.

“Well,” said Mistress Fenn at last. She folded the letter very small. “It seems I am to be a grandmother.”

“I’m glad, mistress.”

“So am I, I think. It will take me a day or two to be sure.” She looked at Wren properly for the first time. “You will want something hot before you go back.”

“I won’t go back tonight,” said Wren. Her eyes stung with salt, and her knees had opinions of their own. “Not with the tide where it is.”

She slept at the Bell, and Juniper slept in the Bell’s stable with her nose in the oats and one ear turned toward the door, in case the fog tried anything. In the morning Hesketh’s chalk would say four hours, or five if she was lucky, and the Saltings would lie out shining between Marrowby and home, as if they had never kept anything at all.
