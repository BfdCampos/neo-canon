Wren Calloway was the only woman in Ferrybridge who would carry the post across the Saltings in March. The men said it was the fog that kept them home. Wren said it was the money that brought her out, which was nearer the truth, and also that somebody had to.

The post was not much, most weeks: a seed catalogue for the vicar, a summons or two, letters from sons at sea to mothers who would carry them unopened to the parson to be read aloud. But it went across every day the tide allowed, and it had gone across every day for eleven years, and Wren was proud of that in a way she would never have said out loud.

The Saltings ran eleven miles from the sea wall at Ferrybridge to the market town of Marrowby, flat as a laid table and twice as treacherous. Twice a day the tide came in across them faster than a horse could trot, and twice a day it went out again and left the old causeway shining like a knife laid on a cloth.

Juniper, the bay mare, had crossed the Saltings more times than Wren could count. She was fourteen, broad in the barrel and short in the temper, and she knew the causeway stones the way a parson knows the psalms. When the water was wrong she would stop, and no kicking on earth would move her. Wren had given up kicking years ago. It was cheaper to trust the mare.

Old Hesketh, a small weathered man, kept the tide-board at the causeway end. Every morning he chalked the safe hours on it in a hand nobody else could read, and every morning somebody asked him what it said.

“You’ve four hours,” he told Wren, without looking up from the board. “Five if you’re lucky. Three if you stop to look at birds.”

“I never stop to look at birds.”

“Your mare does.” Juniper was gazing at a heron in the reeds with the deep suspicion she kept for anything taller than a gull.

Ballast, the piebald gelding, stood dozing at the rail with one hind foot cocked. He belonged to Hesketh, and he had not hurried since the old king died.

Wren’s eyes were the grey of the marsh at first light, and they missed very little. They did not miss the letter Hesketh took from his coat now, sealed in green wax, with a name on it she knew.

“For Mistress Fenn at Marrowby,” he said. “By tonight. She was most particular.”

“She always is.” Wren tucked it inside her coat, against the oilskin packet of the ordinary post, and swung up into the saddle.

“I’ll ride with you as far as the Withy Post,” said Hesketh, and to the surprise of everyone at the causeway end, Ballast included, he did.
