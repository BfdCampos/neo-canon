In March the board sent for her. There was to be an inquiry about the boat, and the keeper was to give an account of the light on the night it went aground.

Jory came to keep the tower while she was gone, though he would not climb it in daylight and only went up at dusk with his eyes shut.

The board sat in a long room above the customs house, under a painting of a ship that had never existed. There were five of them, and a clerk, and a fire that gave no heat at all. They asked about the wick, the oil, the clockwork and the hour, and wrote down everything she said in a book with a green cover.

On the second morning the master of the fishing boat was called, and he told them that he had seen the light all night and had steered by it, and had simply misjudged the tide. He looked at the floor while he said it. The board thanked him and let him go.

Mara, the elder of the two Quells, had always kept the logbook. She took it with her to Dunmore and read from it to the board for an hour, every entry in her own square hand, and when she had finished nobody asked her anything more.

“Your eyes are blue as the bay tonight,” Jory said when she came back, which was what he always said, and was never true.

She stayed in Dunmore two nights at an inn by the market. Then she took the coast road home to Kelsharrow, alone, with the logbook in her coat.
