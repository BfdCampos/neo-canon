The second winter was worse than the first. Storms came in from the west one after another, and twice the sea broke over the rock and put out the fire in the cottage.

Mara kept the light through all of it. On the worst night she sat up in the lamp room with a blanket round her and watched the beam go out over the water and come back. In the morning the bay was flat and white, and a fishing boat lay on its side on the shingle below the cliff.

The village below had lost three roofs and a chapel window. People came up the cliff path afterwards with bread and questions, and she gave them tea and as few answers as she could. Nobody blamed the light. Everybody wanted to hear that it had been burning, and it had.

Mara’s blue eyes narrowed against the glare. She counted the men on the shingle twice before she let herself believe that all of them had come ashore.

Ansel Brisk was among the men who came up from the village to right the boat. He looked at Clover, who had wandered down to the shingle to see what the noise was.

“Clover’s mine by rights,” Ansel said. “Your keeper before you sold her to my father.”

“That Clover of yours is a bull in all but name,” said Teodor, coming up behind him, and the men laughed.

The boat was righted by noon and floated off on the evening tide, and by the next morning there was nothing on the shingle but wrack and a single oar that nobody came back for. Mara carried the oar up to the cottage and leaned it by the door, where it stayed for years.

Mara did not answer either of them. She took the cow home and shut the byre door, and went up the steps to sleep.
