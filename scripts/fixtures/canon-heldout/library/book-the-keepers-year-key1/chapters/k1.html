The light at Kellsharrow stood on a knuckle of black rock above the bay, and Mara Quell was the only woman who had ever kept it. She had kept it for six winters. Mara lived in Kellsharrow, in the keeper’s cottage under the tower, with a stove that smoked and a cow that would not be hurried.

Every evening at dusk she climbed the hundred and twelve steps, trimmed the wick and wound the clockwork that turned the lens. Every morning she climbed them again to put the light out. In between she slept badly and listened to the sea.

The tower had been built a hundred years before by men who did not expect anyone to live in it. The stairs were too steep, the windows too small, and the lamp room so cold in January that the oil thickened in the reservoir. She had learned to warm it with a brick wrapped in sacking, carried up inside her coat.

Ships went by most nights without knowing she was there. That was the point of the light, and she did not mind it. Now and then one of them dipped its own lantern twice as it passed, and she dipped hers back, and that was the whole of the conversation.

The cow was waiting at the gate when she came down that morning. Clover lowered his head and walked into the byre ahead of her, as though the byre had been her own idea. Mara laughed and followed with the pail.

Jory Quell, Mara’s elder brother, came up the cliff path at noon with the post and a sack of oats. He was broad and slow and kind, and he had never once climbed the tower, because the height made him sick.

“You look tired,” he said.

“I am tired,” said Mara. “The lens sticks on the north turn. Something in the clockwork.”

Jory stood beside Teodor Brisk at the rail of the harbour that evening and told him about the lens. Teodor shrugged. He knew horses and cattle, not clocks, and he said so.

Clever as she was, Mara could not mend the lens alone. She wrote to the lighthouse board in Dunmore that night and asked for a man who could.
