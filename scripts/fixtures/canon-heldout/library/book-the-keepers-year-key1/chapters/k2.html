Clover, the red cow, belonged to Mara and to nobody else. She had come with the cottage, thin and lame, and Mara had fed her through a winter that killed half the cattle on the coast. Now she gave more milk than one keeper could drink.

Mara’s eyes were green as bottle glass, and the board’s man noticed them before he noticed anything else. He was young and talked too much, but he found the fault in the clockwork in an hour: a pin worn thin on the north turn.

He stayed three days, mending what else he could find. He oiled the hinges of the lamp room door, rehung the storm shutter on the seaward side and showed her how to listen for the pin before it wore again. When he left he shook her hand like a man shaking hands with another keeper, and she liked him better for it than for anything he had said.

Teodor Brisk, Ansel’s father, kept the farm on the far side of the headland. Hector, Teodor’s black bull, grazed the cliff field there and bellowed at the light every night as it came round.

Ansel Brisk, the younger of the two Brisk men, came over the headland in the afternoon to borrow a lantern. He stayed for supper. He always stayed for supper.

After supper they walked out to look at the bull. Clover had followed them as far as the wall, chewing. Hector shook his horns at the gate and stood there, steaming, until they went away.

Above the headland the sky went the brown of a cow’s flank, and then darker, and the light came on above them, steady now, turning without a catch.
