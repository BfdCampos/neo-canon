Brennock was a grey slate town that climbed the hill above the Sare in three steep streets. The river was brown and fast below the bridge, and in a wet April it came over the meadows as far as the mill.

Dr Haddon had been the town’s doctor for twenty-two years. He was a short, bald man with half-moon spectacles and a voice too big for him, and every morning at eight he drove his old Rover, a green P6 with a cracked windscreen, down Bridge Street to the surgery. He had been born in Leeds and spoke of it as if it were abroad.

His wife, Constance, was a head taller than him, and fair, and she sang in the choir at St Ida’s. She ran the surgery’s books and all of its patients, and the town was more afraid of her than of him.

The surgery was the front half of the Haddons’ house on Bridge Street, a narrow red-brick building with a brass plate by the door. Its waiting room had six hard chairs and a fish tank, and it was always too warm.

On Thursdays the doctor held a clinic at the Corn Exchange, a sandstone hall on the market square with a clock tower that ran ten minutes slow. The clinic was in its Long Gallery, upstairs, a cold panelled room hung with portraits of aldermen, where the light came in through tall windows on the north side.

The vicar, the Reverend Amos Lyle, came to the Thursday clinic for his chest. He was a young, anxious man with red hair, newly come from Durham, and he coughed through every sermon. Mrs Haddon said he would not last a winter.

The doctor’s patients called him the Old Man. Constance called him Arthur, and nobody else did.
