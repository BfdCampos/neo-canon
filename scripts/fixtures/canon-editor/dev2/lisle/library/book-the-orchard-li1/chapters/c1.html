The pickers came to Lisle in the last week of September, as they had every year since Ivo Marchetti was a boy. He stood at the orchard gate with the list and ticked them off: the Dunne family from the coast, two students from Lyon, and old Sander, who had made the cider at Lisle for forty years and was deaf in his left ear.

Ivo was the foreman, a long, stooped man with green eyes and a habit of rubbing the bridge of his nose when he was tired, which was most of the time. He had never touched a drop of the cider, though he could tell a good year from a bad one by the smell of the press-house.

The orchard ran down the south slope of the valley to the river: nine hundred trees, mostly russets, in rows that his grandfather had planted. At the bottom, by the water, stood the press-house, a stone barn with a tin roof and a great oak press that took four men to turn.

His younger sister Tamsin was coming home on the Friday train. She came every harvest, though she had lived in the city for ten years and said she hated apples.

Clem, his grey mare, was waiting at the gate, and he rode her down the rows in the evening light to see how much was left to pick.

Sander caught him at the press-house door. ‘Your sister’ll want the big room,’ he said. ‘She always does.’
