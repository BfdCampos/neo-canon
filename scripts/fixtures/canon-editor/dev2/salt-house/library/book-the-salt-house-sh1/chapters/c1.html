My grandmother’s house stood at the end of Wrack Lane, the last house before the marsh, and every summer of my childhood I was sent there in the second week of July. I was eleven the year this happened. I had my father’s grey eyes and my mother’s habit of saying nothing when I was frightened, and I was frightened of almost everything in that house.

Granny Ruth was my mother’s mother. She was a small, straight-backed woman with white hair she wore in a knot, and she had lived in the Salt House since the day she married. She rose at five every morning to light the range, winter and summer, and she would not have a clock in the kitchen.

My uncle Tobiah lived with her. He was my mother’s younger brother, a big slow man with a beard the colour of rust, and he mended nets for the boats at Saltby. He had lost two fingers of his left hand to a winch when he was twenty. He did not talk much, but on Sundays he took me out along the dyke to look for samphire.

“You’ll be wanting your tea, Nell,” Granny Ruth said, the first evening, and put a plate of bread and dripping in front of me. Nobody else had ever called me Nell. At home I was Eleanor, all of it, always, and I was the only girl in my class who could not swim.

The house itself was brick, low and long, with a slate roof that the gulls walked on in the mornings. It had four rooms downstairs and three up. The back bedroom, which was mine, looked out over the marsh to the sea wall, and its walls sweated in wet weather. The whole house smelt of salt and coal smoke.

Saltby was a mile off along the lane, a fishing village with a chapel, a pub called the Anchor and a harbour that dried out at low tide. On Saturdays Granny Ruth walked there for her groceries, and I carried the basket.

Uncle Tobiah had a dog, a black-and-white collie bitch called Moss. She slept by the range and followed him everywhere, and she would not come to me.

“She’s particular,” Tobiah said. “Like your gran.”

There was one rule in the Salt House, and it was Granny Ruth’s: nobody went out on the marsh after dark. Not Tobiah, not the dog, not me. My mother had told me the same thing in March, before I came, and she had been told it herself as a girl.

In the evenings Granny Ruth read her Bible in Welsh, which she had learnt from her own mother, and Tobiah sat by the window splicing rope. I sat on the rag rug with Moss’s back to me and listened to the wind.
