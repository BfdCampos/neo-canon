The bell went six times, the way it always did, and Hesper was on her feet before the sixth. The floor of the Long Room was stone, and the stone was cold enough to ache. Nobody was allowed slippers before the weighing. Nobody was allowed anything before the weighing.

She stood at the foot of her bed with her arms at her sides and waited. Forty beds ran down the Long Room in two rows, forty girls and boys standing at the foot of them, and not one window between the lot. The light came from a strip of yellow lamps along the ceiling that hummed when they were cold. The air smelled of lye, and of something sweeter underneath, like apples left too long in a drawer.

Hesper was fifteen and the tallest girl in her year, too tall, the warden said, as if it were a habit she could be talked out of. Her hair was cropped close to the skull and fair as wet straw. She kept her hands still. She had learned a long time ago that hands which fidgeted got written down.

At the far end of the room the door unlocked from the outside. It always unlocked from the outside.

The warden came in first. She was a narrow woman in a grey coat buttoned to the throat, and she carried a ring of keys at her hip that she never once looked at. Behind her came the weigher, pushing the scale on its little iron wheels. The wheels squealed on every third turn. Hesper had counted.

“Line,” said the warden.

They lined up by height, which put Hesper near the back, behind Coll. Coll was the nearest thing she had to a friend. He was a head shorter than she was and had black hair that stuck up at the crown however much water he put on it. He was not one of the Spared. He had come up from the valley in a cart two winters ago, with a label on a string round his neck, and he still talked like the valley when he forgot himself.

The “Spared” were the ones born inside. Hesper had been born on the third floor, in the room with the green tiles, and she had never once been further than the outer gate. The Spared did not get labels. They got a number inked on the inside of the left wrist the week they were born. Hesper's was 41.

The rule of the Weighhouse was written above the door in brass letters, and every child could say it in their sleep: a child who falls below the red line three dawns running goes down to the valley, and does not come back up. Nobody said what happened in the valley. Coll, who had come from there, said it was only a town with a mill and too many dogs. Nobody believed him.

The weigher worked down the line. He was an old man, thin as a rake, with spectacles mended at one hinge with thread and ink on his cuffs that never washed out. He hummed while he worked, the same three hymns in the same order, and he wrote every number in a ledger with a green pen. The children called him Brother Ink behind his back. His name, Hesper had once heard the warden say, was Anselm.

“Up,” he said, when it was her turn.

She stepped onto the brass plate. It was colder than the floor. The beam rocked and settled, and the weigher slid the little weight along it with one finger, and peered, and slid it back.

"Hm," he said.

That was all. He wrote something in green. Hesper did not look at the ledger. You did not look at the ledger.

Next came the "gift". The warden poured it from a steel jug into a tin cup, and you drank it standing up while she watched. It was grey and thick and tasted of chalk and pennies, and the Spared had been drinking it every morning of their lives. The valley children were sick on it for their first month. Coll had been sick on it for three.

Hesper drank hers in four swallows and handed the cup back. The warden took it without a word and wiped the rim with a cloth that smelled of camphor.

Then the prick. The weigher took her right thumb between his finger and thumb, and he did it so gently that she hardly felt the needle. A bead of blood came up. He touched a glass slide to it and laid the slide in a wooden tray, in the slot marked with her number.

“Good girl,” he said, and moved on to the boy behind her.

When the line was done the warden clapped once, and they could sit. Hesper sat on the edge of her bed and looked at her thumb. Across the aisle a girl from the valley was crying without making any noise, which was a skill you learned here or did not.

Coll sat down beside her, though it was against the rules to sit on another's bed. He kept his eyes on the door.

"You were light," he said. "I saw his face."

“He always makes that face.”

“Not at you.”

She did not answer. Coll glanced at the door again and then, quick as a card trick, he pressed a crust of bread into her hand. It was hard and grey at the edges and must have been saved from supper, which was also against the rules, which was a great many rules for one crust.

“Eat it after lamps,” he said. “Not before. They’ll smell it on you.”

She closed her fingers over it and put her hand in the pocket of her smock.

The warden was watching them from the door. She had a way of watching that felt like a hand on the back of your neck. Hesper made herself look at the floor until the feeling went.

After the weighing came lessons, and after lessons came the yard, a square of gravel between four high walls where the novices walked in circles for an hour, two by two, without talking. Above the walls there was only sky, white most days, and on clear days the tip of the mountain. Hesper had seen the mountain perhaps twenty times. She thought it looked like a tooth.

She walked with Coll. Behind them the weigher stood in the doorway with his ledger under his arm, humming. When they passed him for the ninth time he said, without looking up, “Forty-one.”

She stopped.

“Two more,” said the weigher, and went inside.

Coll did not say anything until they had turned the corner of the yard. Then he said, very low, "Two more dawns. That's what he means. You've been under twice."

“I know what he means.”

She had known since yesterday. She had felt it in the way her smock hung, in the way her knees looked at night when she pulled her legs up under the blanket. She was not hungry, exactly. She was never hungry here; the gift saw to that. She was only getting less.

That night, after lamps, she lay on her back and listened to the Long Room breathe. Forty children breathing in a room with no windows sounded like the sea, or like what she imagined the sea sounded like. She took the crust out of her pocket and held it on her chest for a long time without eating it.

Then she broke it in half, and ate one half, slowly, and put the other half back in her pocket for the morning.
