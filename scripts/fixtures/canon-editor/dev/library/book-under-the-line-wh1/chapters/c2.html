The bell went six times. On feast days it went twelve, but the feast of the Weighing was a long way off yet, and six was what it gave them.

Hesper was up before the last stroke. Grey light came down through the Long Room’s high windows and lay across the beds in stripes, and for a moment she stood in one of them and pretended it was warm.

Her hand went to her pocket. She still had the half crust the warden had given her, wrapped in the corner of her handkerchief. She ate it now, in three bites, before the door unlocked, and it was the best thing she had ever tasted.

Coll was already in the line. His black hair was flat for once; he had slept on it wet. When she came up behind him he did not turn round.

“I never gave you anything,” he said to the wall, quietly, in case anybody asked.

“I know,” said Hesper.

The weigher came in with the scale. The wheels squealed on every third turn. There was a new ribbon in his ledger, a red one, and Hesper tried not to look at where it lay.

When her turn came Hesper stepped up onto the brass plate. She tucked her black hair behind her ears, as if that would make any difference, and stood very still.

The beam rocked. The weigher slid the little weight along with one finger, and peered, and did not slide it back.

He looked at her over his mended spectacles for a long moment. Then he wrote in the ledger, in green, and said nothing at all, and Hesper stepped down onto the cold stone with her heart going like a bird’s.

On the way out to the yard they passed the infirmary, and through its one window Hesper saw the tip of the mountain, white as a tooth.

In the yard Coll walked beside her without talking, the way they were supposed to. On the ninth circuit he put his shoulder against hers, just for a step.

"What did he write?" he said.

"I didn't look," she said. "You don't look at the ledger."

She thought of Coll pressing the crust into her palm, quick as a card trick, and wondered how many rules it had cost him.
