Nurse Maud Rafferty had worked nights on Larch Ward at St Brannoc’s for nine years. Her shift started at ten every night and ended at six, and she was always early.

Raff knew every patient by the sound of their breathing. She was a tall, rawboned woman of fifty with cropped grey hair and reading glasses on a cord.

Larch Ward was on the second floor of the east wing, a long room of twelve beds with tall windows that would not open.

Mrs Ida Abbott had been in Room 14 for eleven weeks. Room 14 was the single room at the end of Larch Ward, next to the sluice.

At half past ten the desk phone lit up with the night team’s chat.

Okoro: Abbott’s son rang. The barrister. Coming at nine tomorrow

Raff: She’s ninety-one, she won’t remember

Mrs Abbott was eighty-seven, small and sharp, with white hair she wore in a plait. Her son, Gerald, sold insurance in Swindon and had not visited since June.

When Raff went in, Mrs Abbott was awake, very cold and frightened, and Raff fetched a second blanket from the linen cupboard.

Dr Okoro was the night registrar, a soft-spoken man who drank his tea black.
