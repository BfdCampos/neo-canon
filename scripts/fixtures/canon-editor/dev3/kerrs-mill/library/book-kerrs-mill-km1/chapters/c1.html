Absalom Kerr the Elder built the mill race at Hobb’s Ford in the spring of 1871, and for forty years nobody in the valley was allowed to forget it.

The Elder was a broad, slow man with grey eyes and a white beard to his belt. He rose every morning at five and walked the race before breakfast.

His son, Absalom Kerr the Younger, had his mother’s blue eyes and none of his father’s patience. The Younger kept the ledgers and the dog.

The dog was Biscuit, a wire-haired terrier with one torn ear. Biscuit slept under the counting-house stove in winter and on the mill steps in summer, and she would let no one but the Younger lift her. She had bitten the Elder twice.

The Younger’s boy, Absalom the Third, was seven that year. Everyone called him Sal.

The Younger sat late over the ledgers, and Smut, the mill cat, fell asleep on his chest. He was cold and tired, and he did not hear the Elder come in.

Kerr’s Mill stood on the east bank of the Hobb, a tall stone building with a slate roof and eleven windows facing the water.
