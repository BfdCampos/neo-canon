The bell of the Lantern House rang nine times, and Mara Venn was already on the stairs. She was thirty-four, a tall woman with a long dark braid and grey eyes, and she had climbed these three hundred steps every dusk for eleven years. A white scar ran through her left eyebrow. She never spoke of how she got it.

The Lantern House stood at the end of the Dunmere breakwater, a round tower of black stone with eleven floors and a copper lamp-room at the top. Its windows faced east, toward the sea, and the salt had clouded every one of them. Inside it was always cold. The air smelled of lamp oil and wet rope, and the stairs wound up through the middle like the inside of a shell.

Mara kept a brass key on a cord around her neck. It opened the lamp-room and nothing else. She touched it twice at every landing, once for luck and once to be sure, and she did not much believe in luck.

"You are slow tonight," said Tobias, coming down to meet her with a taper in each fist. He was a thin boy of fourteen with red hair and a face full of freckles, and he had been her apprentice since the spring. He grinned at her over the flame.

"I am exactly as slow as I was yesterday," Mara said.

"My mother says the stairs will kill you one day."

"Your mother is dead, Tobias." She said it gently. He shrugged, and she let the lie go, because a boy was allowed one.

The lamp-room was the warmest place in the tower. The great lamp sat in the middle of it on an iron stand, taller than Mara, with a lens of thick green glass that threw its light twelve miles out across the water. She trimmed the wicks herself. She had never let anyone else do it, not even the Harbourmaster.

The Harbourmaster was a heavy man with a grey beard who came out along the breakwater every Friday to count the oil. His name was Edric Slade, though nobody used it. He stood at the door with his hat in his hands and said, "I have never once been to Coldharbour, Mara, and I never will." It was an odd thing to say, and she looked at him a moment too long.

"Nobody asked you," she said.

Mara herself had been born in Coldharbour, in a house above the fish market, and she had left at nineteen with a single bag. She did not like to talk about it. She did not like the sea either, which was a joke the whole town enjoyed, since she lit its lamp every evening of her life.

The Guild of Wick-Trimmers had run the lights of Dunmere for two hundred years. There were forty members, and every one of them wore a grey coat with a brass button at the collar. The Guild met on the first Monday of each month in the cellar of the Salt Cod inn. Mara went to every meeting and said nothing at any of them.

The Guild had one rule above all the others. No lamp may be lit before the dusk bell. It was carved over the door of the Lantern House, and it was the first thing Mara taught Tobias. A lamp lit early burned the oil of the next night, the Guild said, and the next night's oil was the town's last defence against the fog.

That evening the fog came in early. It rolled over the breakwater in a wall, white and silent, and the harbour bells began to ring one after another along the quay. Tobias pressed his face against the green glass.

"Can we light it now?"

"The bell has gone," Mara said. "We light it now."

She struck the flint, and the wick caught. Light poured out of the tower and across the water, and for a moment the fog turned the colour of a pale emerald. She stood with the key in her fist and watched it go out toward the boats.

Down on the quay, a woman from the Guild was counting the lamps along the harbour wall. Her name was Ines Garrow, and she had been a Wick-Trimmer for thirty years. She looked up at the Lantern House and nodded once, the way she always did. Mara nodded back, though nobody could have seen it.

Dunmere itself was a small grey town of slate roofs and crooked chimneys, built on the only flat ground between the cliffs and the harbour. Its people were fishers and rope-makers, and they trusted nothing that could not be mended with tar. The streets ran steeply down to the quay in a dozen narrow stairways, and in winter the whole town smelled of smoke and herring. Mara had never learned to love it. She had learned instead to keep it lit, which she supposed was a kind of love.

Later, when the fog thinned, Mara sat on the top step with a mug of hot cider and Tobias sat beside her. He was quiet for a long while.

"Why do you never go home?" he said at last.

"This is home."

"I meant Coldharbour."

She turned the brass key over in her fingers. "Coldharbour has no lamps," she said. "That is why I came here."

The wind dropped a little. Far out on the water the first boat came in, steering by the green light, and Tobias counted the lanterns on its mast until he fell asleep against her shoulder.
