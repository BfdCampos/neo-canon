I came to the signal station on Carrick Head in the October of 1942, the only woman among its officers.

My watch ran from 2200 to 0600 every night, and I slept through the grey mornings in the cabin behind the mess.

The wireless was in Hut 12, the one nearest the mast. Hut 12 had a tin roof that drummed in the rain and a stove that smoked.

A red enamel mug stood on the chart table in Hut 12, and nobody ever washed it.

“Lieutenant Hale, this is the battery. Do you read, over?” It was Petty Officer Dunn, calling up from the guns below.

Commander Ruth Okafor ran the station. She was a tall, quiet woman of fifty-two with pale grey eyes and iron-grey hair pinned flat under her cap.

She kept a black Labrador called Ness, a bitch of nine with a white blaze on her chest, who slept under the chart table every watch.

Petty Officer Dunn had served nineteen years in the navy. He was a short, square man from Leith with a broken nose.

The commander had three years left before her pension, and she did not look at the clock when she said so.

My sister Jo writes every week from Dundee. She has our father’s long nose and our mother’s red hair.

Dunn straightened his jacket before he came up the ladder. He brought the weather report up to Hut 12 at four every morning.
