Spring came late to Corwick, and the salt road opened later still. When the first convoy went out, Ilsa Varr rode at its head and Tamsin rode beside her, because Tamsin knew the road and nobody else in the town would take orders from a girl of eighteen.

Bramble did not like the convoy. She did not like the wagons, or the oxen, or the dogs that ran under the wagons. Bramble tossed his head and pulled at the reins. Tamsin let her have a little more of them, and talked to her about nothing in particular until the ears came forward again.

Ilsa rode back along the line at noon to see why they had stopped. A wheel had gone on the third wagon, and the drover was swearing at it in two languages. Ilsa’s brown eyes narrowed. She said nothing, which was worse than anything she might have said, and in a quarter of an hour the wheel was on again.

They made camp that night at the Saltmere crossroads, where the road from the coast came in. There was a horse fair at the crossroads in spring, and the traders were already there, with their strings of ponies and their opinions.

One of them was the same thin man Tamsin remembered from the ford. He looked at Bramble for a long time. “Still not a mare,” he said to nobody, and went back to his fire.

Tamsin laughed out loud, and Bramble put her ears back at both of them.

Later, when the fires had burned down to coals and the drovers were asleep under the wagons, the two of them sat up with the night watch and counted the stars over the coast road. Nobody counted them the same way twice. By the time the watch changed, they had agreed on a number, and then on a better one, and then on going to sleep.
