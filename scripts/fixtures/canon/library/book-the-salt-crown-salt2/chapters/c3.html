The coast road took Tamsin eight days. Bramble hated every one of them. She hated the sand, and the gulls, and the grey sea that would not hold still, and on the eighth evening, when they came over the last dune and saw the lights of the harbour, she stopped dead and would go no further.

Tamsin did not argue. She had argued with Bramble for eleven years and had never once won. Tamsin swung down from the stallion and walked the last mile into town on foot, leading the horse, with the ledgers on her own back.

The council’s clerks had given her a room above the customs house, and a table, and more paper than she had ever seen in one place. She worked there from first light until the lamps were lit, and most nights after.

In the mornings she walked down to the harbour wall before the clerks came in, and watched the salt boats unload. The harbourmaster got used to her. By the second week he was keeping a place for her on the bollard nearest the steps, out of the wind, and telling her which of the captains were honest.

Ilsa Varr found her there three weeks later, with the accounts spread over every surface. The captain had ridden from Corwick in five days, which was not possible, and looked it.

They ate by the fire that night, because the room was cold and the lamps were dear. Ilsa’s eyes looked almost brown in the firelight. Tamsin had never noticed before how tired she was.

“The council will want you to stay,” Ilsa said. “They will want you to keep their books for them until you are old.”

“Then they will have to want it,” said Tamsin, and outside in the stable yard Bramble kicked the door, once, as if to agree.
