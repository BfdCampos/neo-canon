Oren met the convoy at Saltmere with twenty riders and a letter from the council. He had learned to fight with his right hand alone, and he fought well enough that nobody at the crossroads remarked on it twice.

He still wore the belt that Ilsa had bound round the stump at the ford. Oren cut the old belt into a strap for his shield once the wrist had healed.

Oren remembered Tamsin binding the stump with her belt, and how her hands had not shaken until afterwards. “Ilsa bound it, not my sister,” he told the riders, and nobody believed him.

The letter was for Tamsin. The council wanted someone to carry the salt accounts to the coast, someone who could read and keep her temper, and they had asked for her by name.

Tamsin, the elder of the two Hales, had always been the one who could read. She read the letter twice by the fire and then handed it to Oren without a word.

Oren read it once. “You’ll go,” he said. It was not a question. “I’ll take the convoy home. Somebody has to see that the salt gets back to Corwick and not to the hill men.”

They argued about it anyway, the way they argued about everything, in short hard sentences with long silences between. The riders pretended not to listen and listened to every word. By the time the moon was up the argument had gone the way both of them had known it would go from the start.

In the morning the convoy turned north, with Oren at its head and Rook dancing under him, and Tamsin turned west with two of the wall guard and a satchel of ledgers. Ilsa watched them both out of sight. Then she took the road back to Corwyck, alone, as she had come.

It was a long road for one rider. The barley was up along the river, green and thin, and the mills that had burned were being built again with new timber that smelled of resin in the sun. At every one of them somebody came out to ask about the salt. She told them what she knew, and rode on.
