Rain had come to Corwick three days early, and the wall walk ran with it. Captain Ilsa Varr was the only woman on the wall that night, and she did not mind the rain. She minded the road.

From the gatehouse the road ran east through the barley and then south toward the ford, a pale thread in the dark. Nothing moved on it. Nothing had moved on it for a week, which was the trouble. The council in the town below had stopped asking her about it, and that was worse than the asking had been.

Captain Varr’s eyes were the grey of wet slate, and they missed very little. She saw the lantern before the watchman did: one light, low and swinging, coming up from the river at a walk.

“Rider,” said the watchman. “One.”

“Two,” said Ilsa. “One of them is walking.”

They came out of the rain together at the foot of the gate: a girl of perhaps seventeen leading a horse, and a young man on foot with a sword on his back. The girl was Tamsin Hale, Oren’s younger sister, and she looked as if she had not slept since the harvest.

Oren Hale did the talking. He always did. He said the ford was clear, that the hill roads were not, and that the men who had burned the mill at Ashby were camped two miles behind them. He said it plainly and without hurry, the way a man reads out a list of prices, and when he was done he waited.

Ilsa listened without moving. When he had finished she opened the gate herself, because the watchman was slow and the rain was not.

“Get your sister under a roof,” she told Oren. “And get that horse out of the weather. Whatever is coming up the road, I would rather meet it dry.”

The gate shut behind them with a sound like a held breath let go. In the yard the horse stood steaming under the lamp, and the girl stood beside it with one hand on its neck, as if she did not trust the town to stay put.

Above them the bell of Corwick began, slow and late, to ring the hour.
