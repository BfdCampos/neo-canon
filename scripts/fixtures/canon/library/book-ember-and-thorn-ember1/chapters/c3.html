They were caught at the ford on the third evening, coming back from Ashby with the salt.

Twelve men came out of the willows on the far side, and the first Tamsin knew of it was Rook screaming. Oren had him turned and was riding at them before anyone else had drawn. The water was high and loud, and for a long moment it was impossible to tell which of the shapes in it were men and which were only the river.

Ilsa Varr held the bank with four of the wall guard. It was over quickly, and badly. When the willows were quiet again, Oren was sitting in the shallows with his sword arm across his knees, and his left hand was gone at the wrist.

Tamsin bound the stump with her own belt. Her hands did not shake until afterwards.

Captain Varr knelt beside him in the water. “You will live,” she said. “You will hate it, but you will live.”

Oren laughed, which frightened his sister more than the blood had. “I have another hand,” he said. “Ilsa, I have another hand.”

Bramble carried him home across her withers, because Rook would not stand for it, and because the grey mare had never in her life been afraid of blood. The salt came home too, all of it, on the backs of the guards’ horses, and that night the whole town ate it in its bread without knowing what it had cost.

The surgeon in Corwick took what was left of the wrist and closed it with a hot iron and twenty stitches. He was not a gentle man, and he did not pretend to be.

By midwinter Oren could saddle Rook one-handed. By spring he could fight that way, after a fashion, and he told everyone who would listen that the left had always been the useless one.
