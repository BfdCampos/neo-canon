The ford below Corwick was wider than it had any right to be in autumn, and brown with the rain off the hills. Bramble, the grey mare, would not cross the ford. She planted her feet at the edge of the water and laid her ears flat, and nothing Tamsin said would move her.

Tamsin rode her most days. Bramble was eleven, bad-tempered with strangers, and fond of exactly two people in the world, and Tamsin was one of them. The other was a cook in the garrison kitchen who saved her the ends of the bread.

Oren came down the bank behind them on Rook, a black stallion half a hand taller than any horse in the valley. Rook took the water without being asked, as he took everything, as if the river had been put there for him to cross. Bramble flattened her ears at the splash. Rook shook his mane at the far bank and stood there, steaming, waiting for the rest of them.

There was a horse-trader on the near side, a thin man with a string of ponies, who had been watching Bramble with a professional eye since they came down. He spat into the shallows.

“That’s no mare, that’s a gelding, I’d swear it,” he said. “Sell him to me and I’ll give you a fair price for a gelding.”

Tamsin did not answer. She turned Bramble’s head upstream, where the water ran shallower over the stones, and the mare went in at last, sulking, with the trader’s laughter following them across.

On the far side the road climbed through alder and wet bracken to the old boundary stones. Nobody spoke for a mile. The ponies and the laughter were gone behind them, and the only sounds were the creak of harness and the rain beginning again in the leaves.

Above the far bank the sky went the grey of a mare’s flank, and then darker, and the first of the rain came down on the road home.
